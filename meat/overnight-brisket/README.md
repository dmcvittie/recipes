# Overnight Brisket (Smoke → Oven Hold)

Full packer brisket smoked for the first half of the cook, wrapped, then finished and held overnight in a low oven. Pull it in the morning, rest it in a cooler, slice at lunch. The long hold is the whole point — the meat sits just under 190°F for hours, which is where collagen actually converts, and it makes the finish time forgiving instead of stressful.

**Total time:** ~26 hours from trim to slicing — 3 hours to temper, ~18 hours of cook, ~5 hours of rest.

## Equipment

- Smoker (pellet grill, offset, whatever holds 180–225°F). _On an analog Traeger you only get 180 and 225 setpoints, which is exactly what this cook needs._
- Kitchen oven
- Leave-in probe thermometer, ideally with an ambient probe plus two meat probes
- Butcher paper, heavy-duty foil, a roasting pan the brisket fits in
- Wire rack and sheet pan
- Dry cooler and old towels
- Foil pan for rendering tallow

## Ingredients

- 1 full packer brisket, 14–16 lbs
- SPG rub — equal parts kosher salt, coarse black pepper, granulated garlic
- The brisket's own trimmings, for tallow

## Timeline

Elapsed time from when the brisket hits the smoker (T+0). Temps are the flat unless noted.

| Elapsed | Stage | Target |
|---|---|---|
| T−3 hrs | Trim, season, rest on a rack at room temp | Surface dry to the touch |
| T+0 | Smoker at 180°F, brisket on | — |
| T+2 | Bump smoker to 225°F | — |
| T+4–5 | Blot pooled juice off the top | Flat ~145°F |
| T+5.5–8 | Stall — bark sets | Flat parks at 150–160°F |
| T+8 | Wrap, into the oven at 250°F | Flat 160–165°F |
| T+11–12 | Drop oven to 220°F for the overnight hold | Flat 185–190°F |
| T+18 | Probe, pull, into the cooler | Flat 188–192°F |
| T+23 | Slice | — |

**To back into a serve time:** count back 23 hours from when you want to slice. A noon serve means the brisket goes on the smoker around 1pm the day before, and gets trimmed and seasoned around 10am. The overnight hold is the slack in the system — it stretches or shortens by hours without hurting anything, so don't panic if the smoke phase runs long.

## Directions

### 1. Trim and season

1. Trim the fat cap to about ¼". Square off the thin edges of the flat and remove the hard fat between point and flat where you can reach it. Save every trimming for tallow.
2. Season all sides heavily with SPG. Heavily — a packer is a lot of surface area and most of this rub becomes bark, not seasoning.
3. Set the brisket on a wire rack over a sheet pan and leave it out at room temperature for 2–3 hours.

This sit does two jobs: it takes the fridge chill off so the cook starts evenly, and it dries the surface so smoke sticks and bark starts forming early instead of steaming. When the rub has gone from grainy and dry to a tacky, dark paste that doesn't rub off on a fingertip, it's ready.

### 2. Render the tallow (during the smoke)

1. Cube the trimmings and pile them in a foil pan.
2. Set the pan on the smoker next to the brisket for the first 3–4 hours. It picks up smoke while it renders.
3. Strain through fine mesh or cheesecloth into a jar. Keep it somewhere warm so it stays liquid for wrapping.

Oven alternative: 250°F, uncovered, 2–3 hours, then strain.

### 3. Smoke — about 8 hours

1. **T+0.** Smoker at 180°F. Brisket on fat side up, thickest part toward the hottest side. Foil under the grate to catch drips. Probes into the thickest part of the flat and into the point.
2. **T+2.** Bump to 225°F. The low start is purely for smoke; 225°F is what renders fat and sets the bark.
3. **Check around T+4 and T+6.** Blot any pooled juice off the top with paper towel. Standing liquid steams the rub underneath it and stalls the bark right where you want it thickest.
4. **Expect the stall from about T+5.5.** The flat parks at 150–160°F for 2–3 hours while evaporative cooling holds it there. This is when the bark forms, so leave the lid alone.
5. **Wrap on bark, not on temperature.** The surface should be dark, matte, and the pepper fused into a crust. Press it — if rub lifts onto your finger, it isn't ready. This usually lands around T+8 with the flat at 160–165°F, but the clock and the thermometer are both secondary to how the bark looks.

### 4. Wrap

1. Brush warm tallow over the flat and across the top of the point.
2. Butcher paper directly against the meat, tucked tight.
3. Two layers of heavy foil around the outside of the paper. Tight, no air pockets, seams up so nothing leaks.
4. Probe back into the thickest part of the flat, straight through the wrap.
5. Set it in a roasting pan.

The tallow is doing real work here. Paper wicks fat and moisture off the surface, and over a long hold that dries the crust out — the layer of fat keeps the bark pliable. Foil-only with tallow is the fallback if the bark still comes out tough.

### 5. Oven finish, then hold — about 10 hours

1. **Oven at 250°F** until the flat reads 185–190°F, roughly 3–4 hours from wrapping. Expect a second short stall in the high 170s; ignore it.
2. **Then drop the oven to 220°F** for the overnight hold. Set a high alarm at 197°F. It probably won't fire. If it does, pull the brisket.
3. The brisket will sit at 186–192°F for six hours or more. That time at temperature is the tenderizing — collagen conversion is time × temperature and it moves fast above 180°F. It does not need to reach 200°F.

**Calibrate the hold setpoint against an ambient probe, not the dial.** Cheap oven thermostats swing ±20°F at low settings, so a 220°F dial can average closer to 205°F in the box. Too low and the flat stalls out in the mid-180s and never finishes; too high and it overshoots. Check the ambient average once before bed and adjust the dial, not your expectations.

### 6. Pull and rest — 4 to 6 hours

1. Probe through the foil into the thickest part of the flat, about 10 hours after wrapping.
2. **Slides in like room-temperature butter** → pull it. Straight into a dry cooler, towels packed around it, lid closed.
3. **Still firm** → bump the oven back to 250°F and check again in 60–90 minutes.
4. **Don't vent it.** After hours at equilibrium there's no carryover heat to bleed off — venting only loses temperature.
5. Leave the cooler closed until you're slicing. Five hours is comfortable, eight still works.

### 7. Slice

1. Separate the point from the flat along the fat seam that runs between them.
2. Flat: against the grain, pencil-thick.
3. Point: rotate 90° — its grain runs differently — and slice thicker, or cube it for burnt ends.
4. Long sharp knife, one pull stroke per slice. No sawing.

## Notes

- **Wrap on appearance, not the clock.** Every number in the timeline flexes except this one. A brisket wrapped before the bark sets never develops one.
- **Blotting the pooled juice mid-smoke** visibly speeds up bark set. Worth the two lid openings.
- **Don't chase 190°F before bed.** Getting to 185–190°F and handing it to the 220°F hold is the plan working, not falling behind.
- **Fat side up works fine** on a pellet grill, where the heat comes from below and off the walls rather than straight up from coals.
- **Tough bark is a moisture problem, not a temperature problem.** Tallow in the wrap is the first fix; foil-only is the second.
- **Plan about ⅓ lb of cooked brisket per person.** A packer loses roughly half its raw weight between trimming and rendering, so a 15 lb brisket feeds 20 or so.

## Timing rules of thumb

- 180°F → 225°F smoke phase: ~8 hours to wrap
- 250°F wrapped in the oven: 3–4 hours to go from 160°F to 188°F
- 220°F hold: as long as you need, within reason
- Cooler rest: 4–6 hours ideal, 8 hours fine
